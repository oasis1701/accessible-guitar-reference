Fretboard guide

Knowing the fretboard means knowing which note sits at every string and fret. This page gives you the raw map, the landmarks that organize it, and the octave shapes that reduce the work: learn two strings well, and the shapes locate every note on the other four. Each string's note table is a level 3 heading, as is each octave shape.

Strings and octave shapes on this page

    How the fretboard is laid out

    Each fret raises a string by one semitone, and there are twelve semitones in an octave, so every string repeats itself at the 12th fret, an octave higher. Between the seven natural notes, five frets carry the sharps and flats; each of those has two names for the same pitch, like F sharp and G flat. There is no sharp or flat between B and C, or between E and F — those pairs sit on neighboring frets.

    Guitar makers mark frets 3, 5, 7, 9, and 12 with dots, and the 12th fret gets a double dot. Sighted players rely on these landmarks constantly, so the tables below mark them on every string. If you memorize only the landmark frets at first, you can reach every other note by counting one fret from a landmark.

    Notes on each string

    Every table runs from the open string to the 12th fret. Sharps and flats are given with both of their names.

    How the strings relate

    Press any string at the 5th fret and you get the same note as the next thinner string played open — except on the 3rd string, where it happens at the 4th fret instead. That one exception is the B string shifting everything by a fret, and it is the same shift you will meet in the octave shapes below.

    A practical plan many teachers give, and the one this page is built around: first learn the 6th string and the 5th string thoroughly, until you can name any fret without counting, because every power chord, barre chord, and most movable shapes take their name from a root on one of those two strings. Then use the octave shapes to find any of those notes again on the thinner strings, instead of memorizing four more strings from scratch.

    Octave shapes

    An octave shape plays the same note in two places at once. Slide it anywhere: whatever the thicker string's note is, the thinner string repeats it an octave higher. These four shapes cover the whole neck.

    You can use them two ways. As pure knowledge, nothing needs to be pressed: the shape simply tells you where a known note sits again, two strings away. As a playable grip, press both notes as described and sound them together — a true octave blends into a single thicker sound, while a miss by a single fret clashes sharply, so your ears confirm every find. And the same distances work from the nut: when the root is an open string there is no finger on it at all, so the open low E finds its octave at the 2nd fret of the 4th string.

    Making the landmarks touchable

    The dots on the fretboard face are visual, and the small side dots are usually inlaid flush, so many blind players add their own tactile markers: a small bump sticker or a short strip of textured tape on the back of the neck or along its edge at frets 3, 5, 7, and 12. A marker at the 5th fret alone already anchors most of what this page teaches. Locating a marker by touch is much faster than counting frets from the nut, especially in the middle of a song.
